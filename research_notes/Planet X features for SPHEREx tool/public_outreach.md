Research date: 2026-11-12. Note on method: spaceappschallenge.org pages (the 2026 challenge statement and the Judging & Awards Guide) could not be fetched directly because of a TLS certificate error in the research environment, so challenge and judging details below come from search-result snippets and secondary/local-event pages. ## 1. Misconceptions (Nibiru, "NASA is hiding it", Planet X vs Planet Nine vs brown dwarfs) and how to frame uncertainty

### Takeaway
NASA frames Planet Nine as explicitly "theoretical", inferred from the clustered orbits of small Kuiper Belt objects, and states that it may not exist at all. Nibiru is a separate internet hoax with no factual basis. A tool should name both of these outright and keep Planet X (Lowell's historical idea), Planet Nine (today's hypothesis) and brown dwarfs (real objects that citizen searches actually find) as separate things.

### Cited Findings
- NASA's Planet X page says "The planet remains theoretical at this point." It also says Batygin and Brown "did not actually observe a planet" and that scientists still debate whether it exists. — [NASA Science: Planet X](https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/planet-x)
- NASA lists the alternative openly. Some researchers think the unusual Kuiper Belt orbits may come from random distribution, and the page adds: "Another possibility is that Planet X (Planet Nine) does not exist at all." — [NASA Science: Planet X](https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/planet-x)
- NASA's estimates: about 5–10 Earth masses, an average orbit 20–30 times farther from the Sun than Neptune's, and an orbital period of 10,000–20,000 years. The page is internally inconsistent on size: it gives both "about 1.5 times Earth's size" and "Neptune-sized". Do not repeat the size claim uncritically. — [NASA Science: Planet X](https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/planet-x)
- Naming, per NASA: "Planet X" comes from Percival Lowell's 1915 hypothesis, later traced to measurement errors. "The 'X' is the letter x, not the Roman numeral for 10." "Planet Nine" is Batygin and Brown's nickname, and any official name would need IAU approval. NASA's page is dated last updated March 12, 2025, so some details (for example Rubin's status) are dated. — [NASA Science: Planet X](https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/planet-x)
- NASA's Planet X page does not mention Nibiru. The tool would have to cover that myth itself. — [NASA Science: Planet X](https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/planet-x)
- In 2017 NASA stated: "Nibiru and other stories about wayward planets are an internet hoax. There is no factual basis for these claims." — reported in [Space.com: Nibiru: The Nonexistent Planet](https://www.space.com/15551-nibiru.html) and press coverage, e.g. [Newshub](https://www.newshub.co.nz/home/world/2017/11/nasa-scientist-debunks-popular-nibiru-conspiracy.html)
- David Morrison ran NASA's "Ask an Astrobiologist" Nibiru/2012 Q&A. By 2009 it had received nearly a thousand questions and he had posted more than 200 answers. — [NASA Ask an Astrobiologist (archived): Nibiru and Doomsday 2012 Q&A](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/eot2008/20090825111320/http://astrobiology.nasa.gov/ask-an-astrobiologist/intro/nibiru-and-doomsday-2012-questions-and-answers); [NPR, 2009](https://www.npr.org/2009/11/15/120436493/scared-of-planet-nibiru-nasa-would-like-to-help)
- Morrison's core rebuttal is about visibility. A large planet heading toward Earth would already be easily seen, and astronomers would have been tracking it for years. The doomsday date first appeared as May 2003 and was moved to December 2012 when nothing happened. — [NASA Ask an Astrobiologist (archived)](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/eot2008/20090825111320/http://astrobiology.nasa.gov/ask-an-astrobiologist/intro/nibiru-and-doomsday-2012-questions-and-answers); [Space.com](https://www.space.com/15551-nibiru.html)
- Morrison also rebutted a specific "NASA found it" claim: the 1983 IRAS infrared survey found many infrared sources, but none was Nibiru or any other outer-solar-system planet. This matters for an infrared mission like SPHEREx, because the same myth pattern ("infrared telescope secretly found it") is likely to resurface. — [NASA Ask an Astrobiologist (archived)](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/eot2008/20090825111320/http://astrobiology.nasa.gov/ask-an-astrobiologist/intro/nibiru-and-doomsday-2012-questions-and-answers)
- The scare had real emotional cost. Morrison and NASA colleagues received thousands of anxious emails as December 21, 2012 approached. — [NPR, 2009](https://www.npr.org/2009/11/15/120436493/scared-of-planet-nibiru-nasa-would-like-to-help); [RIT news](https://www.rit.edu/news/nasa-scientist-says-we-need-stop-worrying-about-apocalypse)
- Fringe and religious outlets explicitly merge Planet Nine news with Nibiru, e.g. a "Nibiru, Planet X or Planet Nine?" end-times headline. Each new Planet Nine headline tends to restart the confusion. — [Christian Post](https://www.christianpost.com/news/nibiru-planet-x-or-planet-nine-new-end-times-prediction-as-nasa-admits-case-for-mystery-planet-is-compelling.html); [Christian Today](https://www.christiantoday.com/news/nibiru-end-times-conspiracy-theories-refuse-to-disappear-as-nasa-concedes-planet-nine-may-be-real)
- Even the hypothesis's authors present it with caveats. Brown concedes that no single line of evidence alone proves Planet Nine exists. — summarized in [Astronomy.com: Does Planet Nine exist?](https://www.astronomy.com/science/does-planet-nine-exist/)
- Current state of the debate (2025–2026): a June 2026 ScienceDaily item, reprinting The Conversation, reports that recent discoveries such as the Subaru-found object 2023 KQ14 complicate the hypothesis. Separately, a 2025 Live Science report covered a "first good candidate" claim. — [ScienceDaily, June 2026](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/06/260608040009.htm); [Live Science](https://www.livescience.com/space/planets/astronomers-identify-first-good-candidate-for-controversial-planet-nine-deep-in-our-solar-system). Treat aggregator claims of "compelling new evidence" in mid-2026 (e.g., voxlogue.vercel.app) as unverified.
- Brown on Rubin Observatory: Rubin will "tell us whether we're crazy or not." This is a useful, honest framing of uncertainty for the public. — via [Live Science / Space.com: Is the search nearly over?](https://www.livescience.com/space/planets/planet-nine-is-the-search-for-this-elusive-world-nearly-over)
- Brown and Batygin's 2016 models pointed to a planet of 5–10 Earth masses as far as ~700 AU away. Brown: "If we find something moving out around 500 to 600 AU, it will be Planet Nine." — [Space.com: Planet Nine search](https://space.com/planet-nine-is-the-search-nearly-over)
- Brown dwarfs are a distinct, real class of object. Backyard Worlds: Planet 9 began as a hunt for the hypothetical planet, but its actual discoveries are brown dwarfs: objects heavier than planets and lighter than stars, many of them near the Sun. — [Wikipedia: Backyard Worlds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Backyard_Worlds); [NASA: Volunteers double known population of brown dwarfs](https://science.nasa.gov/get-involved/citizen-science/nasa-volunteers-double-known-population-of-brown-dwarfs/)
- Explaining false color is a trust issue. Research says image creators should explain how they translate data into color for non-experts. This applies directly to SPHEREx infrared images shown in color. — [Frontiers in Communication, 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/communication/articles/10.3389/fcomm.2024.1288896/pdf)

### Inferences
- Suggested "three names, three things" explainer card:
  - Planet X: Lowell's 1900s idea, based on bad data. Pluto was found during the search but did not explain the anomaly.
  - Planet Nine: a 2016 hypothesis about a planet of roughly 5–10 Earth masses, hundreds of AU away. It is inferred, not seen.
  - Brown dwarfs: real "failed stars" that volunteers have actually found.
  - Nibiru: a hoax.
- The AI guide should have pre-written, sourced answers to common questions: "Is it going to hit Earth?", "Is NASA hiding it?", "Did SPHEREx/WISE/IRAS find it?". The tone should be calm and non-mocking, given how many people wrote to NASA frightened.
- The best counter to "NASA is hiding it" is openness. Show that SPHEREx data is public (see Section 4) and let users look themselves.
- Uncertainty language to use consistently: "hypothetical", "if it exists", "astronomers are testing the idea". Show the competing explanation (random clustering or observational bias) next to the hypothesis. Never call a user-flagged moving dot "Planet X". Call it a "candidate moving object" and explain how a real candidate gets confirmed (follow-up observations, orbit fitting, Minor Planet Center).
- Make one point explicitly: an object hundreds of AU away moves extremely slowly and is very faint, so the absence of a detection is not evidence of a cover-up.

### Gaps
- I could not find a current (2026) NASA page dedicated to Nibiru. The best primary sources are Morrison's archived FAQ and the 2017 NASA statement as quoted in the press.
- I found no published study measuring how common the Planet Nine / Nibiru confusion is among the public, such as survey percentages.
- I found no primary-source predicted apparent brightness (magnitude) for Planet Nine in the time available. Check Brown & Batygin's papers, e.g. arXiv:1902.10103 "The Planet Nine Hypothesis", before quoting a number.

## 2. Exemplary interactive visualizations and what makes them effective

### Takeaway
The most effective precedents are a few simple, direct-manipulation interactions: blinking two images to see motion (Tombaugh's comparator, Lowell's blink simulation, the Backyard Worlds flipbook) and scrolling through real scale (If the Moon Were Only 1 Pixel). Each puts the user in the discoverer's seat and makes an abstract quantity (motion, distance) something they experience.

### Cited Findings
- Tombaugh's blink comparator was a Zeiss-built microscope. A dial flipped a mirror between views of two photographic plates of the same star field, so a moving object appeared to jump while stars stayed fixed. — [Smithsonian NASM: Finding Pluto with the Blink Comparator](https://airandspace.si.edu/stories/editorial/finding-pluto-blink-comparator)
- The discovery plates were exposed January 23 and January 29, 1930, and Pluto was found in February 1930. The plates were 14 x 17 inches, contained thousands of stars, and were compared over "weeks and months". Pluto "moved too slowly to be an asteroid". — [Smithsonian NASM](https://airandspace.si.edu/stories/editorial/finding-pluto-blink-comparator)
- Lowell Observatory's archive holds sections of the original discovery plates with arrows marking Pluto's movement. Its 2015 "Pluto at 85" exhibit let guests discover Pluto with a computer simulation that "blinks" through the discovery plates. The comparator was lent to the Smithsonian in 2015 and later returned to Flagstaff for Lowell's Pluto exhibit. — [Lowell Observatory Archives: Pluto Discovery Plates](https://collectionslowellobservatory.omeka.net/items/show/1247); [Lowell Archives: The Discovery](https://collectionslowellobservatory.omeka.net/exhibits/show/pluto/the-discovery); [Lowell.edu](https://lowell.edu/?p=3046)
- Backyard Worlds: Planet 9, launched in February 2017 on Zooniverse and funded by NASA, lets anyone flip through WISE/NEOWISE infrared images spanning many years and look for objects that move between frames. This is a modern digital blink comparator using infrared survey data, the closest analog to a SPHEREx tool. — [Wikipedia: Backyard Worlds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Backyard_Worlds); [Zooniverse project results](https://www.zooniverse.org/projects/marckuchner/backyard-worlds-planet-9/about/results)
- Josh Worth's "If the Moon Were Only 1 Pixel" sets one pixel equal to the Moon's diameter (about 3,475 km). Users scroll horizontally through mostly empty space at true scale. It has jump-to-planet buttons and a mode that auto-scrolls at the speed of light, and the empty space is filled with wry commentary. — [Josh Worth](https://joshworth.com/jpw/?p=2700); [Universe Today](https://www.universetoday.com/articles/if-the-moon-were-only-one-pixel-a-scale-model-of-the-solar-system)
- A press estimate says the 1-pixel map would need about 665 monitors laid end to end to show everything at once. — [PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/gaming-industry/this-tediously-accurate-map-of-our-solar-system-with-the-moon-scaled-to-a-single-pixel-would-still-take-665-monitors-laid-end-to-end-to-show-all-at-once)
- The U!Scientist museum tabletop adapted Zooniverse's Galaxy Zoo into a multi-person exhibit and was evaluated for how it supported group discussion of real research. — [U!Scientist paper (NSF PAR)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10283491)

### Inferences
- Core interaction for the SPHEREx tool: a blink/flipbook toggle between two SPHEREx epochs (about 6 months apart), with an optional "difference" view. Frame it explicitly as "Tombaugh's machine, rebuilt for the infrared".
- A "to-scale scroll" or "light-time" widget (Sun → Neptune → Planet Nine's ~400–700 AU) could borrow the 1-pixel model's humor-in-the-void style to show how far and how lonely the region is.
- What makes these work: one simple verb (blink, scroll), real data or true scale, the user does the discovering, and humor or narrative in the empty spaces.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve specifics on NASA's Eyes on the Solar System features (e.g., whether it shows Sedna or Planet Nine orbits or has guided tours), Caltech/Batygin–Brown's published orbit animations, standalone Planet Nine orbit simulators, or planetarium shows on Planet Nine. These still need research.
- I found no evaluation data (learning outcomes) for the Lowell blink simulation or the 1-pixel model. Their effectiveness here is inferred from their design and popularity, not measured.

## 3. Storytelling hooks (Neptune, Lowell's Planet X, Tombaugh, Sedna, detective framing, scale)

### Takeaway
The search has a natural detective arc: Neptune predicted on paper and found within a night, Lowell's Planet X chased because of bad data, Pluto found by blinking plates, and Planet Nine inferred from orbital clues and now being hunted. Telling it honestly includes the false leads, which models good skepticism.

### Cited Findings
- Le Verrier presented his prediction to the French Academy in August 1846. Galle in Berlin found Neptune the same evening he searched, about 1° from Le Verrier's predicted position. Adams's independent prediction was about 12° off. — [Astronomy.com: Who discovered Neptune?](https://www.astronomy.com/science/who-discovered-neptune)
- Arago said Le Verrier found Neptune with "the point of his pen". Credit was disputed: Challis had seen Neptune twice without recognizing it, and Adams and Le Verrier eventually shared credit. — [Astronomy.com](https://www.astronomy.com/science/who-discovered-neptune); [Physics Today PDF](https://aip.brightspotcdn.com/PTO.v72.i3.70_1.online.pdf)
- The prediction was less exact than the legend suggests. Le Verrier's inferred orbit was wider and more eccentric than Neptune's real one, and his mass estimate (~36 Earth masses) was about double the real value (~17). — [Batygin et al., "The Planet Nine Hypothesis," arXiv:1902.10103](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1902.10103)
- Brown and Batygin explicitly invoke the Neptune analogy and admit its limits. Brown wrote they "failed to match" Le Verrier and Galle's one-night discovery. — [Physics World: Pathway to Planet Nine](https://physicsworld.com/a/pathway-to-planet-nine/); [arXiv:1902.10103](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1902.10103)
- Cautionary note: after Neptune, later claims of extra planets came mostly from misread data, with Lowell's Planet X as the classic case. — [Physics World](https://physicsworld.com/a/pathway-to-planet-nine/); [NASA Science: Planet X](https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/planet-x)
- The Tombaugh/Pluto discovery story (blinking plates over weeks and months, January 1930 plates) is covered in Section 2. — [Smithsonian NASM](https://airandspace.si.edu/stories/editorial/finding-pluto-blink-comparator); [Planetary Society: Clyde's Search](https://www.planetary.org/articles/pluto-discovery-4)
- Scale and slowness, per NASA: an average orbit 20–30x farther than Neptune's and a 10,000–20,000-year orbit. — [NASA Science: Planet X](https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/planet-x)
- The evidence is a "connect-the-dots" pattern. Live Science described astronomers narrowing Planet Nine's location "by playing massive game of 'connect the dots'", and Scientific American framed it as "its hiding places are running out". — [Live Science](https://www.livescience.com/space/planets/astronomers-narrow-down-where-planet-nine-could-be-hiding-by-playing-massive-game-of-connect-the-dots); [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/where-is-planet-nine-its-hiding-places-are-running-out/)

### Inferences
- Suggested narrative chapters for the AI guide or onboarding:
  1. "A planet found with a pen" (Neptune, 1846)
  2. "The planet that wasn't" (Lowell's Planet X, from measurement errors)
  3. "Blink!" (Tombaugh, 1930: the user does a blink exercise here)
  4. "Clues in the ice" (clustered Kuiper Belt orbits, Planet Nine 2016)
  5. "Your turn" (SPHEREx images)
- Pairing the Neptune success with the Lowell failure is the most honest way to frame Planet Nine as "could go either way".
- Scale hooks: 10,000–20,000-year orbit, so it moves only a tiny amount across the sky in 6 months. At hundreds of AU, sunlight takes days to arrive. The light-time figure should be calculated by the team from AU distance (1 AU ≈ 8.3 light-minutes) rather than quoted from an unsourced figure.

### Gaps
- Barnard's Star (high proper motion as an analogy for "things that move") and Sedna (discovery story, extreme orbit) were not researched in the time available. Find primary sources (e.g., NASA, Caltech Mike Brown pages) before using them.
- The Planetary Society Pluto series (parts 1–4) was found but not read in full. Specifics such as the number of plates or stars Tombaugh examined remain unverified.

## 4. Citizen science and interactive-learning research (guided tours, progressive disclosure, aha moments, gamification, injected test objects)

### Takeaway
Volunteers are mostly motivated by contributing to real science. Injected synthetic signals ("sims") with immediate feedback are an established way to train users, measure detection sensitivity and give the satisfying "I found one" moment, provided they are disclosed and taper off. Feedback must be timed carefully, and guided learning strongly shapes engagement.

### Cited Findings
- A survey of nearly 11,000 Galaxy Zoo volunteers found that their primary motivation is a desire to contribute to scientific research. — [Raddick et al. 2013, "Galaxy Zoo: Motivations of Citizen Scientists," arXiv:1303.6886](https://arxiv.org/abs/1303.6886)
- Planet Hunters injects simulated transits into real data. Per the PHT II paper, the sims help "assess the sensitivity of the project, training the users and providing immediate feedback, and to gauge the relative abilities of individual users". — [Planet Hunters TESS II, arXiv:2011.13944](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.13944)
- Users are always told after classifying a simulated light curve whether they spotted the injected signal. The sim rate drops from 30% for a user's first 10 classifications to 1% by 100 classifications. — [arXiv:2011.13944](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.13944)
- The original 2011 Planet Hunters blog explained that sims test which planets volunteers can detect, so the project can estimate the fraction of planets it misses. — [Planet Hunters blog: Simulated Transits](https://blog.planethunters.org/2011/01/10/simulated-transits/)
- Volunteer reaction to sims was mixed. Some said they were useful for calibrating their own performance, others asked for fewer ("Fewer sims please?"), and a moderator noted users are not supposed to catch every sim. — [Planet Hunters Talk: Simulated transits?](https://talk.planethunters.org/boards/BPH0000002/discussions/DPH0001aln); [Planet Hunters Talk: Fewer sims please?](https://talk.planethunters.org/boards/BPH0000002/discussions/DPH0001l7g/)
- A 2019 Oxford impact evaluation of Planet Hunters: 74% of respondents learned about astronomy and 66% enjoyed learning through the project. Reported drawbacks were tedium, repetition, and lack of individual feedback and recognition. — [Planet Hunters blog: Exploring the impact of Planet Hunters](https://blog.planethunters.org/2020/01/27/exploring-the-impact-of-planet-hunters-key-findings/)
- Backyard Worlds shows that public discovery is real. Twenty citizen scientists were co-authors on a 2020 paper reporting ~100 cool brown dwarfs, and NASA later reported that the project's volunteers helped find more than 3,000 such objects, doubling the known population. Its first discovery, WISEA 1101+5400, was announced in 2017. — [Space.com](https://space.com/citizen-scientists-discover-95-brown-dwarfs.html); [NASA](https://science.nasa.gov/get-involved/citizen-science/nasa-volunteers-double-known-population-of-brown-dwarfs/); [Sci.News](https://www.sci.news/astronomy/backyard-worlds-planet-9-cold-brown-dwarf-04919.html)
- Museum research: open-ended immersive exhibits draw visitors but can make learning harder. Feedback that is "too complicated, or delivered too early or too late, can disrupt engagement". — [NSF PAR paper on open-ended exhibits](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10110547)
- A Chalmers thesis on astronomy exhibits found that "design features, guided learning, and accessibility of interaction strongly influence" whether visitors meaningfully engage. — [Chalmers ODR thesis](https://odr.chalmers.se/bitstreams/30e0a00f-b798-4b28-aaaf-acd924674a64/download)
- Classic exhibit research identifies technological novelty and open-endedness as traits that hold visitor attention. — [Sandifer 2003, JRST](https://tigerweb.towson.edu/csandife/jrst2003.pdf)

### Inferences
- "Find the planted planet" tutorial: inject one or more synthetic slow-moving sources into SPHEREx epoch pairs. Have the AI guide coach the user until they find it, then reveal "That was a test object we planted; here's how real candidates are vetted." Disclose up front that some objects are practice. Taper the frequency, following Planet Hunters' 30% → 1% schedule. This delivers a guaranteed "aha" without misleading anyone.
- Address the recognition gap Planet Hunters volunteers reported. Give personal feedback, such as streaks or badges tied to skills ("spotted a test object at difficulty 3"), not to fake discoveries.
- Progressive disclosure: beginner mode (blink + guide narration) → explorer mode (choose sky regions, adjust contrast) → expert mode (spectral bands, coordinates, export to MPC/IRSA tools). The guide should give short, timely feedback and avoid long lectures, in line with the museum research.
- Frame participation as contributing to the real search (the top motivation), and be honest about what the tool can and cannot discover.

### Gaps
- I found no controlled study of whether injected sims increase or decrease long-term volunteer retention.
- I found no specific research on "progressive disclosure" or beginner/expert modes in astronomy tools. Recommendations here draw on general museum feedback research.
- I did not find whether Backyard Worlds uses synthetic injected movers in its tutorial.

## 5. Accessibility and inclusivity (language, color-blind palettes, mobile, low bandwidth)

### Takeaway
NASA's strongest accessibility precedent in astronomy is data sonification, developed with blind and low-vision communities, and research suggests multisensory data increases trust and access. I found no NASA color-blind or mobile guidelines specific to visualization, so the team should apply general web standards and document its choices.

### Cited Findings
- NASA's Chandra X-ray Center leads data sonification as a NASA's Universe of Learning partner. It adapted JWST images to sound with blind and visually impaired community members. — [CfA/Harvard: JWST images set to sound](https://pweb.gws.cfa.harvard.edu/news/jwsts-first-full-color-images-data-are-set-sound); [Phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2022-08-webb-full-color-images.html)
- Sonification can map color, brightness, star positions or spectral absorption features to sound. — [Phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2022-08-webb-full-color-images.html)
- A 2024 Frontiers in Communication study of blind/low-vision and sighted listeners found that multisensory astrophysical data "could establish additional avenues of trust, increase access, and promote awareness of accessibility". — [Frontiers in Communication 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/communication/articles/10.3389/fcomm.2024.1288896/pdf)
- The same body of research urges creators to explain false-color translation to non-experts. — [Frontiers in Communication 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/communication/articles/10.3389/fcomm.2024.1288896/pdf)
- SPHEREx observes in 102 infrared wavelengths, all invisible to the eye, so any color rendering is a translation. — [JPL: SPHEREx's First All-Sky Map](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/images/pia26600-spherexs-first-all-sky-map/)

### Inferences
- Possible features:
  - A sonified "blink" where a moving object produces a changing tone
  - Alt-text and AI-guide narration of each image
  - A visible "What do these colors mean?" toggle
  - Grayscale or perceptually uniform, color-blind-safe colormaps for difference images (avoid red/green diffs)
  - Keyboard-operable blink controls
- For mobile and low bandwidth: tile or cutout images (small JPG/PNG previews before full FITS), lazy loading, and a lightweight text mode for the AI guide.
- Language: plain-language glossary terms (AU, infrared, proper motion) in the guide, and multilingual support, since Space Apps is global and the AI guide can translate.

### Gaps
- I found no NASA-published color-blind-safe or accessibility guidelines specific to astronomy visualization, and no accessibility documentation for NASA Eyes on the Solar System.
- I found no research on mobile or low-bandwidth use of citizen-science astronomy tools, such as the share of Zooniverse traffic from mobile.
- Colormap recommendations (e.g., viridis/cividis) and WCAG contrast standards are general best practice and were not sourced in this session. Cite WCAG 2.2 and colormap literature if they are used in the submission.

## 6. Space Apps judging criteria, the 2026 "Planet X and SPHEREx" challenge, and past winners

### Takeaway
Space Apps judges on five equal criteria: Impact, Creativity, Validity, Relevance and Presentation. The 2026 challenge asks for a public web tool showing SPHEREx near-infrared images so people can spot objects that move or change over time. Recent global winners paired real NASA data with a clear human purpose.

### Cited Findings
- The five official criteria are Impact, Creativity, Validity, Relevance and Presentation. Guidance stresses that Presentation deserves equal attention even though teams tend to focus on the first four. — [Space Apps Judging & Awards Guide (via search snippet)](https://www.spaceappschallenge.org/resources/judging-awards-guide); [Project Submission Guide](https://www.spaceappschallenge.org/resources/project-submission-guide/)
- Older (2020) criterion definitions, which may be reworded since:
  - Impact: how much good the project could do and how many people it could reach
  - Validity: scientifically sound, does what it claims, works
  - Relevance: actually responds to the chosen challenge
  — [2020 Space Apps team page quoting criteria](https://2020.spaceappschallenge.org/challenges/create/create-mascot/teams/curious-explorers/stream); [2022 Judging and Awards Guide PDF](https://spaceapps-prod.s3.amazonaws.com/media/documents/Space_Apps_2022_Judging_and_Awards_Guide.pdf)
- The 2026 challenge "Planet X and SPHEREx" exists at spaceappschallenge.org/2026/challenges/planet-x-and-spherex/. Local-event listings summarize it as a "public web tool displaying near-infrared sky images to discover shifting celestial objects", rated advanced difficulty and tagged astrophysics, planets and moons, and software. — [Official challenge page (title only retrieved)](https://www.spaceappschallenge.org/2026/challenges/planet-x-and-spherex/); [Nepvents listing](https://www.nepvents.com/events/cmvp131cy0000qtidqmehjx9h); [HackAthlone challenges](https://hackathlone.com/challenges)
- A NASA release describes the 2026 astrophysics challenges as focused on public access to mission data and tools that show sky changes over time, citing SPHEREx. — [NASA Science: Space Apps welcomes all ages](https://science.nasa.gov/universe/nasa-welcomes-all-ages-experience-levels-for-space-apps-challenge/)

### Inferences
- How each criterion maps to this project:
  - Validity: honest uncertainty framing (Section 1), real SPHEREx/IRSA data, and injected test objects that measure detection sensitivity, like Planet Hunters
  - Impact and Most Inspirational: connection for ordinary people, multilingual/accessible AI guide, myth-busting Nibiru anxiety
  - Creativity: AI guide that drives the blink viewer, "Tombaugh's machine rebuilt" storyline, sonified motion
  - Relevance: directly showing change over time between ~6-month SPHEREx epochs
  - Presentation: a short demo in which a viewer finds a planted moving object in under a minute
- The 2025 Best Use of Data winner was AI-assisted analysis of NASA astronomy data, which suggests judges reward AI that does real work on mission data, not just a chatbot overlay.
