# Planet Nine / Planet X: State of the Science (as of November 2026), for the SPHEREx Tool

Research date: 2026-11-12. Planet Nine (P9) has **not** been discovered or confirmed. Every candidate so far is unconfirmed. Some low-quality sites claim it was "officially confirmed" in 2026. That claim is false; see Q3.

---

## Q1. What is the evidence for Planet Nine, and what are the main counterarguments?

### Takeaway
The case for P9 rests on indirect dynamical evidence: distant Kuiper Belt orbits (a > ~250 AU) appear clustered in orientation, some perihelia are "detached" from Neptune, and there are highly inclined or retrograde TNOs. Selection-bias analyses (Napier et al. 2021) find no statistically significant clustering. Discoveries in 2025, including 2017 OF201 and 2023 KQ14 "Ammonite", add outliers and push any P9 farther out. The hypothesis is still **contested and unproven**. Its proponents say no alternative explains all the effects.

### Cited Findings
- The Batygin et al. 2019 review (Physics Reports, arXiv:1902.10103) gives three lines of evidence: (1) clustering of distant KBO orbits with a ≳ 250 AU; (2) perihelia of some KBOs detached from Neptune; (3) the origin of highly inclined and retrograde long-period orbits. The authors say these "cannot be accounted for by interactions with the known eight-planet solar system alone." — [Batygin et al. 2019, arXiv:1902.10103](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10103)
- The proponents have continued publishing. One example: "Generation of Low-Inclination, Neptune-Crossing TNOs by Planet Nine" (Batygin et al., 2024) argues that P9 explains a population of low-inclination Neptune-crossers. — [arXiv:2404.11594](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.11594)
- **Counterargument: selection bias.** Napier et al. 2021 ("No Evidence for Orbital Clustering in the Extreme Trans-Neptunian Objects") modelled 14 ETNOs from DES, OSSOS and the Sheppard–Trujillo survey using each survey's pointing history, depth and tracking selection. They found "this sample provides no evidence for angular clustering." The data are consistent with a uniform population at the 17%–94% level, depending on the parameter. — [Napier et al. 2021, arXiv:2102.05601](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.05601)
- **2017 OF201** (Cheng, Li, Yang et al.; announced by the MPC on 21 May 2025): a dwarf-planet candidate about 700 km across (size assumes an albedo), currently about 90 AU from the Sun, with an orbital period of about 25,000 yr. Its orbit is an outlier to the clustering. Simulations show it stays stable without P9, but with P9 it could be ejected within about 100 Myr. Co-author Yang cautioned that this is "not necessarily a death knell" for P9. — [arXiv:2505.15806](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.15806v2); [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/science/space/planet-nine-not-quite-astronomers-think-spotted-new-dwarf-planet-rcna210448); [Astronomy.com](https://www.astronomy.com/science/does-our-solar-system-have-a-new-dwarf-planet)
- **2023 KQ14 "Ammonite"** (Chen et al., Nature Astronomy, 14 July 2025, FOSSIL survey with Subaru, CFHT follow-up in July 2024): a sednoid with perihelion of about 66 AU, found near 71 AU. It is the fourth known sednoid. Its orbit is oriented differently from the other sednoids, and simulations show it has been stable for at least 4.5 Gyr. The study "neither confirms nor disputes" P9 but constrains it: if P9 exists, it is likely beyond about 500 AU. — [ASIAA / Academia Sinica release, 2025-07-15](https://www.sinica.edu.tw/en/news_content/55/3226); [Subaru/NAOJ](https://www.cfca.nao.ac.jp/en/node/1877); [ScienceDaily (The Conversation), June 2026](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/06/260608040009.htm); [Smithsonian](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/astronomers-discover-ammonite-a-mysterious-distant-object-that-could-shed-light-on-our-solar-systems-history-180987019/)
- The orbital elements a ≈ 252 AU, e ≈ 0.739 and i ≈ 11° for 2023 KQ14 come only from a Wikipedia mirror, so treat them as unverified. — [Wikipedia mirror](https://kompas.wasmer.app/https_en_wikipedia_org/wiki/2023_KQ14)
- Proponent view: Mike Brown (2024), quoted in the June 2026 coverage: "I think it is very unlikely that P9 does not exist," and "There are currently no other explanations for the effects that we see." — [ScienceDaily, June 2026](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/06/260608040009.htm)
- **Alternative "Planet Y" hypothesis** (Siraj, Chyba & Tremaine, MNRAS, 21 Aug 2025): a bias-corrected measurement of the Kuiper Belt mean plane shows a local warp. They attribute it to an undiscovered, relatively small, inclined planet at roughly 100–200 AU. This is **separate from P9**. A secondary source quotes 0.06–1 Earth masses and i ≥ 10°, which could not be verified against the paper. — [EarthSky](https://earthsky.org/space/planet-y-hiding-solar-system-kuiper-belt/); [Eos](https://eos.org/articles/a-survey-of-the-kuiper-belt-hints-at-an-unseen-planet); [ScienceAlert](https://www.sciencealert.com/planet-y-a-hidden-earth-size-world-could-lurk-far-closer-than-planet-nine); [CNN via KTVZ](https://ktvz.com/cnn-other/2025/10/03/planet-y-astronomers-find-fresh-clues-of-hidden-world-in-our-solar-system/)

### Inferences
- In the tool, present P9 as "a hypothesis supported by indirect orbital evidence, actively debated." Do not call it a discovery. Show both the clustering plot and the selection-bias rebuttal.
- The trend from 2025 to 2026 is that new extreme objects (OF201, KQ14) add outliers and push a viable P9 to larger distances. That makes it fainter and slower-moving, so harder to find.
- Do not merge "Planet Y" (about 100–200 AU, roughly Mercury-to-Earth mass) with P9 (about 400–800 AU, about 5–10 Earth masses). They are different hypotheses with different evidence.

### Gaps
- The following well-known items were **not verified with fetched sources in this session**. Check them before quoting in the tool:
  - Sedna (Brown, Trujillo & Rabinowitz 2004) and 2012 VP113 (Trujillo & Sheppard 2014, Nature 507, 471): the inspiration for the inner-Oort/sednoid population.
  - 2015 TG387 "Leleleku" (Sheppard et al. 2019, AJ 157, 139): perihelion about 65 AU, a ≈ 1,000+ AU.
  - The original P9 paper (Batygin & Brown 2016, AJ 151, 22).
  - The idea that P9 tilts the Sun's spin axis by about 6° (Bailey, Batygin & Brown 2016).
  - The selection-bias critique from OSSOS (Shankman et al. 2017).
  - Self-gravitating massive TNO disk as an alternative (Sefilian & Touma 2019).
  - The primordial black hole idea (Scholtz & Unwin 2020, PRL).

## Q2. Current best-estimate parameters and expected appearance (including SPHEREx 0.75–5 µm)

### Takeaway
The current reference estimate (Brown & Batygin 2021) is about 6.2 Earth masses, a ≈ 380 AU, q ≈ 300 AU and i ≈ 16°, with a long tail to larger distances. The predicted reflected-light V is roughly 21–24+. Intrinsic heat would raise its temperature to roughly 40–50 K, versus about 10 K from sunlight alone, but its near-IR flux is far fainter than SPHEREx's single-band depth. **SPHEREx is very unlikely to detect P9 directly** (inference; no SPHEREx team statement found).

### Cited Findings
- **Brown & Batygin 2021 ("The Orbit of Planet Nine"):** mass 6.2 (+2.2/−1.3) M⊕; semi-major axis 380 (+140/−80) AU; perihelion 300 (+85/−60) AU; inclination 16 ± 5°. P9 is "closer and brighter than initially expected," with a long tail toward larger distances. Radius and albedo uncertainties could make it fainter. — [arXiv:2108.09868](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.09868)
- **Batygin et al. 2019 review range:** 5–10 M⊕, a ≈ 400–800 AU, e ≈ 0.2–0.5, i ≈ 15–25°. — [arXiv:1902.10103](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10103)
- **Thermal and photometric models** (Linder & Mordasini 2016), nominal case of 10 M⊕ at 700 AU:
  - Effective temperature 47 K, versus about 10 K equilibrium, because of internal heat.
  - Radius 3.7 R⊕.
  - Magnitudes: V = 21.7; WISE W1 = 20.1, W2 = 20.1, W3 = 18.6, W4 = 10.2 (Vega).
  - Reflected-light V near aphelion: 24.3 (5 M⊕), 23.7 (10 M⊕), 23.3 (20 M⊕), 22.6 (50 M⊕).
  - Intrinsic emission peaks in the far/mid-IR (Q band), not the near-IR. Larger masses would likely already have been caught by past IR surveys.
  — [arXiv:1602.07465](https://arxiv.org/abs/1602.07465)
- **Predicted sky region:** after the ZTF, DES and Pan-STARRS searches, most remaining parameter space is at V > 21, near where the **northern galactic plane crosses the ecliptic**. Crowded star fields there make detection hard. — [Brown, Holman & Batygin 2024, arXiv:2401.17977](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.17977)
- The 2025–2026 sednoid results (2023 KQ14) suggest any P9 probably lies beyond about 500 AU. — [ScienceDaily, June 2026](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/06/260608040009.htm)
- **SPHEREx specifications** (Bock et al., mission paper, arXiv:2511.02985, accepted ApJ):
  - 102 spectral channels from 0.75 to 5.0 µm, resolving power R = 35–130.
  - 6.2″ pixels.
  - 5σ depth after mapping the full sky four times over two years: 19.5–19.9 AB per channel for 0.75–3.8 µm (R ≈ 40), and 17.8–18.8 AB for 3.8–5.0 µm (R ≈ 120).
  — [arXiv:2511.02985](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.02985)

### Inferences
- **Converting the WISE predictions:** using the standard Vega→AB offsets (W1 +2.70, W2 +3.34), the Linder & Mordasini nominal planet is about 22.8 AB at 3.4 µm and about 23.4 AB at 4.6 µm. That is roughly 3–5 mag (about 15–100 times) fainter than SPHEREx's per-channel 5σ depth. Even stacking all channels (a gain of about 2.5 mag at best, ideal case) leaves it marginal at most.
- **Reflected light** (V ≈ 21–24) is also below SPHEREx's depth. An ice-giant atmosphere would have strong methane absorption in the near-IR (roughly 1.7, 2.3 and 3.3 µm), making the reflected spectrum even darker in parts of the SPHEREx band. This is general planetary-science knowledge, not verified with a P9-specific source here.
- **Suggested tool wording:** "SPHEREx is an excellent tool for learning how searches for moving objects work, and it catalogs many solar system bodies. Planet Nine, if it exists, is likely too faint for SPHEREx to detect directly. Deep optical surveys such as Rubin/LSST are the primary hope."
- **Display numbers:** show parameters as ranges with uncertainties, for example "~5–10 Earth masses; ~400–800 AU (best fit ~380–500 AU); i ~15–25°; V ~21–25; T_eff ~40–50 K." Do not show a single precise position.

### Gaps
- No published SPHEREx-specific P9 detectability study was found.
- No updated post-2024 best-fit orbit was found. The Pan-STARRS paper may contain an updated distribution, but the abstract did not state one.
- Diameter and albedo assumptions behind V magnitudes vary by paper.

## Q3. Recent searches and candidates (2016–2026)

### Takeaway
Optical surveys (ZTF, DES, Pan-STARRS1) have excluded about 78% of the Brown & Batygin parameter space to V ≈ 21.5. A 2025 far-IR IRAS–AKARI candidate (Phan et al.) remains unconfirmed, with no reported follow-up detection. Rubin's first data (April 2026) found about 380 new TNOs, including two with aphelia near 1,000 AU, but no P9. LSST is the key upcoming test.

### Cited Findings
- **Brown, Holman & Batygin 2024 (AJ), Pan-STARRS1 DR2:** rules out a P9 with the Brown & Batygin 2021 characteristics to a 50% completeness depth of V = 21.5. Combined with ZTF and DES (which together had excluded about 61%), **78% of parameter space is excluded**. The team injected more than 50,000 synthetic P9s to test completeness. Brown named Rubin/LSST as the survey most likely to find it. — [arXiv:2401.17977](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.17977); [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/where-is-planet-nine-its-hiding-places-are-running-out/); [Live Science](https://www.livescience.com/space/planets/astronomers-narrow-down-where-planet-nine-could-be-hiding-by-playing-massive-game-of-connect-the-dots)
- **Phan et al. 2025 (PASA; arXiv:2504.17288), IRAS (1983) and AKARI (2006):**
  - Method: they searched for far-IR sources that moved over 23 years (expected motion about 3′/yr), using the AKARI Monthly Unconfirmed Source List.
  - Selection: pairs separated by 42′–69.6′, corresponding to 500–700 AU and 7–17 M⊕.
  - Result: 13 pairs, reduced to **1 good candidate** after inspection. The IRAS source is absent at that position in AKARI, and vice versa.
  - The authors say the two detections cannot determine an orbit and that follow-up is needed. Phan estimated that the candidate, if real, would be more massive than Neptune.
  - Critics note it could be noise.
  — [arXiv:2504.17288](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.17288); [Sky at Night Magazine](https://www.skyatnightmagazine.com/news/planet-9-iras-akari-survey); [Universe Today](https://www.universetoday.com/articles/is-this-the-first-hint-of-planet-nine)
- As of October 2026, the candidate still lacks a series of observations showing orbital motion, so it is **not a confirmed detection**. Phan presented related posters at NTHU events in March and May 2026, but no confirmation has been reported. — [abovethenormnews (low-quality aggregator), Oct 2026](https://www.abovethenormnews.com/2026/10/10/planet-nine-new-evidence/); [NTHU indico 2026](https://indico.phys.nthu.edu.tw/event/188/contributions/912/)
- **Rubin Observatory first data** (NOIRLab, 2 April 2026): more than 11,000 new asteroids, about 380 new TNO candidates found in under two months (versus about 5,000 known TNOs found over three decades), and 33 new NEOs. Two objects, **2025 LS2** and **2025 MX348**, have aphelia around 1,000 AU, among the 30 most distant known minor planets. Kevin Napier said such objects may show "whether a hitherto undiscovered 9th large planet may still be out there." — [EarthSky, 5 Apr 2026](https://earthsky.org/space/11000-new-asteroids-rubin-obervatory-380-tnos/); [GeekWire 2026](https://www.geekwire.com/2026/rubin-observatory-11000-new-asteroids/)
- **LSST start date conflict:** EarthSky (April 2026) said LSST would begin "later this year." An aggregator says it began in late June 2026. The exact date was not verified with a primary source. — [EarthSky](https://earthsky.org/space/11000-new-asteroids-rubin-obervatory-380-tnos/)
- **Earlier context:** Rubin's team said that if a large distant TNO exists, Rubin should be able to detect it. — [NPR, Apr 2025](https://www.npr.org/2025/04/09/nx-s1-5347688/planet-nine-vera-rubin-observatory)
- **Other searches:** a TESS shift-stacking search of the galactic plane (2020) and a 2018 algorithmic search did not detect P9. — [TESS search, arXiv:2010.13791](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.13791); [arXiv:1805.01203](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1805.01203)
- **Citizen science:** the "Backyard Worlds: Planet 9" volunteers (WISE data) reported doubling the known population of nearby brown dwarfs (May 2026). This is a side result, not a P9 detection. — [Phys.org, May 2026](https://phys.org/news/2026-05-planet-volunteers-population-brown-dwarfs.html)
- **Misinformation alert:** a site titled "Scientists have officially confirmed discovery of Ninth Planet" (June 2026) is not supported by any reputable source. Several "Planet Nine 2026" blogs and a "voxlogue" page make unverified claims. — [example false claim](https://news.sciandnature.com/2026/06/scientists-have-officially-confirmed.html)

### Inferences
- A "search history" timeline for the tool could read:
  - 2016: hypothesis proposed.
  - 2021: refined orbit.
  - 2021–2024: ZTF, DES and Pan-STARRS exclusions (78%).
  - 2025: IRAS–AKARI candidate, 2017 OF201, 2023 KQ14, Planet Y.
  - 2026: Rubin first TNOs, LSST begins.
  - Status: undetected.
- The Phan candidate illustrates the same method the tool would teach: compare two epochs and look for a source present in one and absent in the other at the expected offset. It is also a teaching example of why two epochs alone are not enough. You need three or more to fit an orbit.

### Gaps
- No primary-source confirmation of the LSST official start date.
- No 2026 peer-reviewed follow-up of the Phan candidate (for example DECam or Subaru imaging) was found.
- No Rubin P9 constraint paper was found as of November 2026.

## Q4. Historical precedents and Nibiru vs. science

### Takeaway
Neptune (1846) is the success story of predicting a planet from gravitational anomalies, the model P9 follows. Lowell's "Planet X" search led to Pluto through blink comparison, but Lowell's prediction was based on spurious Uranus residuals. Nibiru is a debunked doomsday hoax with no scientific basis.

### Cited Findings
- **Neptune:** Le Verrier predicted Neptune's position from Uranus's orbital irregularities. Galle and d'Arrest at the Berlin Observatory found it on 23 September 1846 (some sources say the night of 23–24 September), within about 1° of Le Verrier's position, near the Capricornus/Aquarius border. Adams's prediction was about 12° off. Challis had unknowingly recorded it earlier. — [APS News](https://www.aps.org/apsnews/2020/08/neptunes-existence-confirmed); [NASA History](https://www.nasa.gov/history/175-years-ago-astronomers-discover-neptune-the-eighth-planet); [Astronomy.com](https://www.astronomy.com/science/who-discovered-neptune)
- **Lowell's Planet X:**
  - From August 1905, Lowell started the first systematic photographic search for "Planet X." Nearly 1,000 plates examined under a blink comparator gave negative results. — [EBSCO Research Starters](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/lowell-predicts-existence-pluto)
  - **Pluto:** Clyde Tombaugh, using the Zeiss blink comparator to alternate between plates of the same field taken on 21, 23 and 29 January 1930, spotted a moving dot on 18 February 1930 near Lowell's predicted region. Lowell Observatory announced the discovery on 13 March 1930. Pluto was reclassified as a dwarf planet in 2006. — [Lowell Observatory archives](https://collectionslowellobservatory.omeka.net/exhibits/show/pluto/the-discovery); [APS News](https://www.aps.org/apsnews/2009/03/tombaugh-discovery-pluto-1930); [Astronomy.com](https://www.astronomy.com/today-in-the-history-of-astronomy/feb-18-1930-discovery-of-pluto/)
- **Nibiru:** NASA states, "Nibiru and other stories about wayward planets are an Internet hoax. There is no factual basis for these claims." The doomsday date has moved repeatedly (2003, 2012, 2017, 2018). A real approaching planet would have been tracked for years and would be visible to the naked eye. NASA distinguishes it from the Caltech P9 hypothesis. — [Snopes](https://www.snopes.com/nasa-warns-nibiru-is-headed-straight-for-earth/); [eWeek, NASA 2012](https://www.eweek.com/news/nasa-working-to-dispel-2012-apocalypse-myth/); [Inverse](https://www.inverse.com/article/36640-nibiru-planet-x-september-23-hoax)

### Inferences
- Tombaugh's blink comparator is the direct ancestor of the tool's "compare SPHEREx epochs" feature. A blink or flicker UI is historically apt.
- Pluto turned out far too small to cause the Uranus "residuals" Lowell used. Its discovery near the predicted spot was largely fortunate. This is a good lesson on prediction vs. luck, though it was not explicitly sourced in this session.

### Gaps
- Barnard's Star (discovered by E. E. Barnard in 1916; highest proper motion of any star, about 10.3″/yr) was not verified with a fetched source this session.
- The Uranus-residuals and Pluto-mass explanation (resolved after Voyager 2's 1989 Neptune mass measurement) was not sourced here.

## Q5. How fast would a planet at 300–800 AU move on the sky? Formulas for the tool

### Takeaway
For a distant planet, the apparent motion seen from Earth is **dominated by parallax** from Earth's own orbit: a yearly loop with semi-amplitude of about 206265″/d (d in AU), or about 7′ at 500 AU. The true orbital motion is only about 1–4′ per year. Over hours to days, the motion near opposition is about 4–11″/day (retrograde), which is enough to show up in images taken days to weeks apart.

### Cited Findings
- Phan et al. expected P9 motion of about 3′/yr and used a 23-year IRAS–AKARI baseline. Their pair separations of 42′–69.6′ correspond to 500–700 AU in their model. — [arXiv:2504.17288](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.17288)
- Tombaugh detected Pluto (about 40 AU) as a shift between plates taken days apart. — [Lowell Observatory archives](https://collectionslowellobservatory.omeka.net/exhibits/show/pluto/the-discovery)

### Inferences (derived from standard Kepler and parallax physics; computed here, not quoted from a source)
- **Annual parallax (reflex) semi-amplitude:** p ≈ 206265″ / d[AU].
  - 300 AU: 688″ (11.5′)
  - 500 AU: 413″ (6.9′)
  - 800 AU: 258″ (4.3′)
  
  The object traces an ellipse of this semi-major axis each year. It is flattened by ecliptic latitude β (semi-minor axis ≈ p·sin β).
- **Heliocentric orbital angular rate (circular approximation):** ω ≈ 360° / d^1.5 per year (P = d^1.5 yr).
  - 300 AU: 0.069°/yr ≈ 249″/yr ≈ 4.2′/yr
  - 500 AU: 0.032°/yr ≈ 116″/yr ≈ 1.9′/yr
  - 800 AU: 0.016°/yr ≈ 57″/yr ≈ 1′/yr
  
  For an eccentric orbit, the rate at radius r is ω = √(GM·a(1−e²)) / r², which is slower near aphelion. Phan's quoted ~3′/yr is faster than the circular value at 500–700 AU, so their model presumably assumes eccentric-orbit speeds and/or includes parallax between epochs. Treat this as a caveat.
- **Instantaneous sky rate at opposition** (Earth's motion dominates):
  
  rate ≈ 3548″/day × (1 − d^−½) / (d − 1)
  
  (3548″/day is Earth's mean heliocentric angular rate.)
  - 300 AU: about 11″/day (0.47″/hr)
  - 500 AU: about 6.8″/day (0.28″/hr)
  - 800 AU: about 4.3″/day (0.18″/hr)
  
  Motion is retrograde (westward) near opposition. A simple approximation: rate ≈ 3548″/d per day. A rough upper bound is about 148″/hr divided by d.
- **Detectability baselines for SPHEREx (6.2″ pixels):**
  - At 500 AU, the object moves about one SPHEREx pixel per day near opposition. Within a single exposure it is effectively stationary (no trailing).
  - Between SPHEREx's roughly six-monthly all-sky passes, the parallax shift is up to about 2p (about 14′ at 500 AU), which is about 130 pixels. It would appear as a "new" source at one epoch and be "missing" at the other, like the Phan IRAS–AKARI approach.
  - Linking it requires a predicted path, or shift-and-stack along trial orbits.
- **Distance from parallax:** if a source's 6-month displacement Δθ (arcsec) is measured near quadrature/opposition geometry, then d ≈ 2 × 206265 / Δθ AU (rough, for an ecliptic-plane object). This is a good interactive "estimate the distance" widget.
- **Brightness scaling** for reflected light: flux ∝ R²·albedo / (r²·Δ²) ≈ 1/d⁴, so doubling the distance makes it 16× fainter, which is 2.5·log₁₀16 ≈ 3.0 mag. Thermal flux from internal heat scales only as 1/Δ², which is why far-IR surveys (IRAS, AKARI) were tried.

### Gaps
- No published educational formula sheet specific to P9 sky motion was found. The values above are standard derivations and should be double-checked (for example against JPL Horizons for Sedna at about 83 AU as a sanity check).

## Q6. SPHEREx's relevance: cadence, depth, ability to detect P9, team statements

### Takeaway
SPHEREx (launched 11 March 2025) maps the whole sky four times in two years, in 102 channels from 0.75 to 5 µm, with 6.2″ pixels, to about 19.5–19.9 AB (5σ) per channel. That is excellent for asteroid and comet spectroscopy but far too shallow for a P9 at V ≈ 21–25. **No SPHEREx team or NASA statement was found claiming SPHEREx can or will search for P9.** Its solar-system role is a catalog of roughly 10⁵ asteroids and other small bodies.

### Cited Findings
- **Mission parameters:** 102 bands from 0.75 to 5.0 µm, R = 35–130, 6.2″ pixels. 5σ depth is 19.5–19.9 AB (0.75–3.8 µm) and 17.8–18.8 AB (3.8–5.0 µm) after four full-sky maps over two years. Launch was 11 March 2025. — [Bock et al., arXiv:2511.02985](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.02985)
- **Solar system science goals:** the SPHEREx Solar System Object Catalog will cover hundreds of thousands of solar system objects at roughly NEOWISE sensitivities. Goals include sizes, albedos and compositions for about 10⁵ asteroids and finding newly active asteroids. The catalog will support planetary defense. — [arXiv:2402.08705](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.08705); [EPSC 2024 abstract](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EPSC2024/EPSC2024-619.html); [Universe Today](https://www.universetoday.com/articles/a-new-space-telescope-will-map-the-universe-and-help-protect-the-earth-from-asteroids)
- A targeted search returned no SPHEREx publication on TNOs or P9. — (search result, Nov 2026; no source to cite)

### Inferences
- **Appropriate scientific framing for the challenge tool:**
  - SPHEREx multi-epoch images let users practice moving-object detection, using blink and flicker, parallax and shift-and-stack.
  - They can recover known bright moving objects (asteroids, possibly the brightest TNOs or dwarf planets such as Pluto, Eris and Makemake, which are V ≈ 14–19). This needs checking against SPHEREx depth and source confusion.
  - They can provide infrared spectra that distinguish ices and minerals.
  - A P9 at V ≈ 21–25 is about 2–5 mag below SPHEREx's single-channel depth.
- **Most useful science features for accuracy:**
  - P9 parameter ranges with error bars.
  - The 78% excluded region and the remaining region near the galactic plane/ecliptic crossing.
  - A parallax and motion calculator using the Q5 formulas.
  - A clear "contested/unconfirmed" label.
  - A misinformation box separating Nibiru from P9.
- The 6.2″ pixels mean source confusion in the galactic plane will be severe. That is the same region where the remaining P9 parameter space concentrates.

### Gaps
- Exact SPHEREx revisit cadence per sky position: commonly described as about every 6 months, with denser coverage near the ecliptic poles. This was not verified beyond "4 full-sky maps in 2 years."
- No NASA/IPAC public statement on SPHEREx and Planet Nine was found.
- Whether SPHEREx can detect the brightest known TNOs (Eris, Makemake, Haumea) per channel was not verified.
