# Let visitors run the Planet Nine hunt

The strongest "Planet X" section for your entry does not promise a planet. It hands visitors the real method: blink two SPHEREx epochs about six months apart and turn the shift into a distance. It sets that method inside the true story of Neptune, Lowell, Tombaugh and today's debate, and it is honest that **SPHEREx is very unlikely to detect Planet Nine directly**. The hypothetical planet is predicted at reflected-light V ≈ 21–25. SPHEREx's all-sky per-channel 5σ depth is about 19.5–19.9 AB, and no SPHEREx team statement claims it can find the planet ([Bock et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.02985); [Brown & Batygin 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.09868)). That honesty is the differentiator. Competing entries already ship blink viewers, photometry, spectra and AllWISE frames. What they lack is a parallax-distance calculator built around SPHEREx's quadrature geometry, a "find the planted planet" tutorial with disclosed test objects, a built-in vetting checklist driven by SPHEREx's own pixel flags, and a calm myth-buster that separates Planet Nine from Nibiru. Backyard Worlds: Planet 9 volunteers point to the same design. Their power users left the Zooniverse flipbook for WiseView-style controls and one-click catalog checks. Credit, not gamification, keeps the 1% who do most of the work ([Meisner et al. 2024](https://theoryandpractice.citizenscienceassociation.org/articles/10.5334/cstp.737)). SPHEREx can offer those volunteers something WISE cannot: a low-resolution 0.75–5 µm spectrum that shows the methane trough of a cold brown dwarf.

## SPHEREx almost certainly cannot see Planet Nine, and saying so is the feature

As of November 2026, Planet Nine is **an unconfirmed hypothesis supported by indirect orbital evidence and actively disputed**. The case rests on three clues: distant Kuiper Belt orbits (a ≳ 250 AU) that appear clustered, perihelia "detached" from Neptune, and highly inclined or retrograde objects. Its proponents say these "cannot be accounted for" by the known eight planets alone ([Batygin et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10103)). The main rebuttal is selection bias. Napier et al. modelled each survey's pointing and depth for 14 extreme TNOs and found "no evidence for angular clustering" ([Napier et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.05601)). Discoveries in 2025 complicated the picture further. 2017 OF201 has an orbit that P9 could destabilize ([arXiv:2505.15806](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.15806v2)). The sednoid 2023 KQ14 "Ammonite" has been stable for 4.5 Gyr and pushes any P9 beyond about 500 AU ([ASIAA](https://www.sinica.edu.tw/en/news_content/55/3226); [ScienceDaily, June 2026](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/06/260608040009.htm)). Mike Brown still says "There are currently no other explanations for the effects that we see" ([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/06/260608040009.htm)). A separate "Planet Y" proposal puts a smaller inclined planet at roughly 100–200 AU to explain a warp in the Kuiper Belt plane. The tool should keep it distinct from P9 ([EarthSky](https://earthsky.org/space/planet-y-hiding-solar-system-kuiper-belt/); [Eos](https://eos.org/articles/a-survey-of-the-kuiper-belt-hints-at-an-unseen-planet)).

The search history gives the "your turn" moment real stakes. ZTF, DES and Pan-STARRS1 together excluded **about 78% of the predicted parameter space** down to V ≈ 21.5, using more than 50,000 injected synthetic planets to measure completeness. The remaining space sits mostly at V > 21, where the **northern Galactic plane crosses the ecliptic** ([Brown, Holman & Batygin 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.17977)). WISE excluded a thermal P9 brighter than W1 = 16.7 (Vega) over most of the high-latitude sky ([Meisner et al. 2018](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1712.04950)). A 2025 IRAS–AKARI far-infrared candidate has no confirming orbit ([Phan et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.17288)). Rubin's first data found about 380 new TNOs, two with aphelia near 1,000 AU, but no planet ([EarthSky, Apr 2026](https://earthsky.org/space/11000-new-asteroids-rubin-obervatory-380-tnos/)). Brown's line that Rubin will "tell us whether we're crazy or not" is a ready-made, honest frame for the public ([Live Science](https://www.livescience.com/space/planets/planet-nine-is-the-search-for-this-elusive-world-nearly-over)).

The detectability verdict depends on which thermal model you pick, and the two research strands disagree on how bad things look. The Linder & Mordasini nominal planet (10 M⊕ at 700 AU, W1 = W2 = 20.1 Vega) converts to roughly 22.8 AB at 3.4 µm and 23.4 AB at 4.6 µm. That is 3–5 magnitudes below SPHEREx's per-channel depth ([Linder & Mordasini 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1602.07465)). The brightest thermal models reach W1 = 16.1 Vega, or about 18.8 AB, which is comparable to a single SPHEREx survey's band-4 depth ([Meisner et al. 2018](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1712.04950)). But WISE has already ruled out that bright end at high Galactic latitude. The only plausible window left is the crowded Galactic plane, where SPHEREx's **6.2″ pixels** make source confusion severe ([Bock et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.02985)). The correct public line is the one the science notes propose: SPHEREx is excellent for learning how moving-object searches work, it catalogs many solar-system bodies, and Rubin/LSST is the primary hope for P9 itself. The SPHEREx solar-system catalog targets about 10⁵ asteroids, which will be the most common real "movers" users meet ([arXiv:2402.08705](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.08705)).

Misinformation needs its own module, not a footnote. NASA calls the planet "theoretical at this point" and openly says it "does not exist at all" is a possibility ([NASA Science: Planet X](https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/planet-x)). The same NASA page does not mention Nibiru, so the tool has to cover it. NASA's 2017 statement calls Nibiru "an internet hoax" with "no factual basis" ([Space.com](https://www.space.com/15551-nibiru.html)). David Morrison's archived Q&A gives the calm core rebuttal: a planet approaching Earth would already be easy to see and long tracked. It also rebuts the specific claim that "an infrared telescope secretly found it", which IRAS faced and which SPHEREx will face too ([NASA Ask an Astrobiologist, archived](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/eot2008/20090825111320/http://astrobiology.nasa.gov/ask-an-astrobiologist/intro/nibiru-and-doomsday-2012-questions-and-answers)). Thousands of frightened people emailed NASA before December 2012, so the AI guide's tone should be reassuring, never mocking ([NPR, 2009](https://www.npr.org/2009/11/15/120436493/scared-of-planet-nibiru-nasa-would-like-to-help)). A "three names, three things" card answers the confusion directly. Planet X was Lowell's idea, built on bad data, and "the 'X' is the letter x, not the Roman numeral for 10" ([NASA](https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/planet-x)). Planet Nine is today's inferred hypothesis. Brown dwarfs are real objects that volunteers actually find. Nibiru is a hoax. The best answer to "NASA is hiding it" is to show that the SPHEREx data are public through IRSA and let users look for themselves ([JPL PIA26600](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/images/pia26600-spherexs-first-all-sky-map/)). It also helps to warn that 2026 "officially confirmed" headlines are false ([example](https://news.sciandnature.com/2026/06/scientists-have-officially-confirmed.html)).

## A six-month gap turns SPHEREx into a parallax ruler

SPHEREx's survey design hands you a distance calculator that no competing feature list mentions. The observatory flies a dawn–dusk Sun-synchronous orbit and looks about 90° from the Sun. It covers the full sky about every six months, and each position gets all 102 channels within roughly two weeks per survey ([Bock et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.02985v2)). Every field is therefore seen near quadrature, once in the "morning" sky and once in the "evening" sky, with Earth displaced about 2 AU between the two views. The researchers derive that a distant object shifts by **Δθ ≈ 412,530″ / d[AU]**, or about 67,080/d SPHEREx pixels. That is ~134 pixels at 500 AU and ~67 pixels at 1,000 AU. Within one two-week visit, the same geometry leaves the object almost still: about 3″ (half a pixel) of reflex motion at 500 AU. These are the researchers' own derivations from standard parallax physics, not published SPHEREx figures, and the team should check them against JPL Horizons. The design consequence is clear: **stack channels within a visit, then blink or shift-and-stack across visits** under a distance hypothesis.

| Distance (AU) | Shift between 6-month visits | SPHEREx pixels | Heliocentric drift per 6 months (circular) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 100 | ~1.15° | ~670 | — |
| 380 (P9 best fit) | ~1,086″ | ~177 | ~14 px |
| 500 | ~825″ | ~134 | ~9 px |
| 1,000 | ~413″ | ~67 | — |
| ~67,000 | ~6″ | ~1 | — |

*All rows are the researchers' derivations (parallax semi-axis 1 AU/d, flattened by sin β at ecliptic latitude β; orbital rate ≈ 1,296,000″/a^1.5 per year). They are not quoted from a source.*

For the public, this becomes a slider: "drag the distance and watch where the dot would jump". Add a reverse mode where the user measures a shift and reads off a distance. For astronomers, it becomes a trial-orbit overlay: draw the predicted parallax ellipse for a chosen distance and test whether a pair of detections falls on it. A random direction relative to the ecliptic ellipse is an immediate red flag. The same arithmetic delivers a lesson most viewers never get. A star 10 parsecs away has 0.1″ of parallax, so **stars essentially never move a SPHEREx pixel in six months**. Anything that jumps between SPHEREx epochs is a solar-system object or an artifact. That explains why SPHEREx alone is a poor brown-dwarf motion finder. The researchers add that a 1″/yr star moves only about 2–3 pixels over the ~15 years between WISE and SPHEREx (their own arithmetic). So long-baseline proper-motion work needs WISE or unWISE frames alongside SPHEREx.

A brightness slider should sit next to the motion slider, because brightness is the real limit. Reflected light falls as roughly 1/d⁴, so doubling the distance costs about **3.0 magnitudes**, or 16× fainter. Thermal light from internal heat falls only as 1/d², which is why far-infrared searches such as IRAS–AKARI were tried ([Phan et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.17288)). One note says "2.5 mag per doubling". That conflicts with its own formula, since 10·log₁₀2 ≈ 3.0, so use 3.0. Draw horizontal lines for SPHEREx depth per band and let users raise them by co-adding. White-noise stacking gains about 1.25·log₁₀(n) magnitudes, so co-adding 17 channels of one band adds roughly +1.5 mag (researcher's estimate; real gains are smaller because of confusion). Users can then see for themselves how far the planet falls short. A scale widget in the style of Josh Worth's "If the Moon Were Only 1 Pixel" can show the emptiness of 500 AU with humour ([Josh Worth](https://joshworth.com/jpw/?p=2700)). At 1 AU ≈ 8.3 light-minutes, sunlight takes about three days to reach 500 AU. The team should compute that figure rather than quote it, as the outreach notes advise.

## Storytelling and planted objects deliver the "aha" without faking a discovery

The history supplies a detective arc that also teaches skepticism, because it includes both a triumph and a false lead. Le Verrier predicted Neptune "with the point of his pen", and Galle found it within about 1° of the predicted spot on the first night of searching ([Astronomy.com](https://www.astronomy.com/science/who-discovered-neptune); [APS News](https://www.aps.org/apsnews/2020/08/neptunes-existence-confirmed)). Even that legend has caveats. Le Verrier's mass estimate (~36 Earth masses) was about double the real value, and Brown and Batygin admit they "failed to match" a one-night discovery ([arXiv:1902.10103](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1902.10103); [Physics World](https://physicsworld.com/a/pathway-to-planet-nine/)). Lowell's Planet X was chased because of measurement errors. Clyde Tombaugh then found Pluto in 1930 by flipping a Zeiss blink comparator between plates taken days apart ([Smithsonian NASM](https://airandspace.si.edu/stories/editorial/finding-pluto-blink-comparator); [Lowell Observatory archives](https://collectionslowellobservatory.omeka.net/exhibits/show/pluto/the-discovery)). The suggested five chapters are "A planet found with a pen", "The planet that wasn't", "Blink!", "Clues in the ice" and "Your turn". They map directly onto an AI guide that drives the viewer. In the "Blink!" chapter the guide hands the user the controls of "Tombaugh's machine, rebuilt for the infrared". Lowell Observatory's own "Pluto at 85" exhibit used a blink simulation of the real plates, which is a proven precedent ([Lowell Archives](https://collectionslowellobservatory.omeka.net/exhibits/show/pluto/the-discovery)).

Planted test objects are the single most valuable addition for ordinary visitors. They solve the core problem: real SPHEREx skies will rarely contain anything exciting. Backyard Worlds tells newcomers to expect **about 60 classifications before spotting a known mover** ([BYW FAQ](https://www.zooniverse.org/projects/marckuchner/backyard-worlds-planet-9/about/faq)), and that is too long for a demo or a casual visit. Planet Hunters shows how to inject synthetic signals honestly. The sims "assess the sensitivity of the project, training the users and providing immediate feedback". Users are always told afterwards whether they caught one. The sim rate drops from **30% of a user's first 10 classifications to 1% by 100** ([Planet Hunters TESS II](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.13944)). Volunteer reaction was mixed: some valued the calibration, while others asked for "fewer sims" ([Planet Hunters Talk](https://talk.planethunters.org/boards/BPH0000002/discussions/DPH0001l7g/)). So disclose up front that practice objects exist, and taper them. SPHEREx makes the technique scientifically faithful. Each cutout carries the detector's own PSF in its PSF HDU, so a planted mover can be drawn with the real instrument's blur ([IRSA cutouts tutorial](https://caltech-ipac.github.io/irsa-tutorials/spherex-cutouts/)). Planting a P9-like object at the parallax offset the calculator predicts ties the whole experience together. The user finds it, the guide reveals it was a test, and then shows how a real candidate would be vetted and reported. Injection-recovery is also exactly how the professionals measured their 78% exclusion ([Brown, Holman & Batygin 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.17977)), so the tutorial doubles as a lesson in how science quantifies "we looked and didn't find it".

Engagement research shapes the delivery. Galaxy Zoo's survey of nearly 11,000 volunteers found that the main motivation is contributing to real science ([Raddick et al. 2013](https://arxiv.org/abs/1303.6886)). The tool should therefore frame users as practising the real search, and say plainly what it can and cannot discover. Planet Hunters' impact evaluation found that 74% of respondents learned astronomy. The drawbacks were tedium and a lack of individual feedback ([Planet Hunters blog](https://blog.planethunters.org/2020/01/27/exploring-the-impact-of-planet-hunters-key-findings/)). That argues for skill-based acknowledgements such as "spotted a test object at difficulty 3", never badges for fake discoveries. Museum studies warn that feedback "too complicated, or delivered too early or too late, can disrupt engagement" ([NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10110547)). So the guide should speak in short, timely turns instead of lectures. Progressive disclosure follows naturally: a guided blink for beginners, an explorer mode with region choice and contrast, and an expert mode with channels, coordinates and exports. That ladder is an inference from general exhibit research, not a tested astronomy result. Finally, celebrate by-catch. Backyard Worlds never found Planet 9, but its volunteers found more than 3,000 brown dwarf candidates ([NASA, May 2026](https://science.nasa.gov/get-involved/citizen-science/nasa-volunteers-double-known-population-of-brown-dwarfs/)). Asteroids, comets and variable sources should count as wins.

Accessibility can be a creative differentiator rather than a checkbox. NASA's Chandra team developed data sonification with blind and low-vision communities ([CfA](https://pweb.gws.cfa.harvard.edu/news/jwsts-first-full-color-images-data-are-set-sound)). A 2024 study found that multisensory astronomy data "could establish additional avenues of trust, increase access" and urged creators to explain false colour ([Frontiers in Communication 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/communication/articles/10.3389/fcomm.2024.1288896/pdf)). All 102 SPHEREx channels are invisible to the eye, so a "What do these colours mean?" toggle is a trust feature ([JPL](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/images/pia26600-spherexs-first-all-sky-map/)). Backyard Worlds considered its colour-inversion button for colour-blind users worth describing in its launch paper ([Kuchner et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/html/1705.02919.pdf)). A sonified blink, in which a moving source changes pitch, would be distinctive. Colour-blind-safe colormaps and WCAG contrast are general best practice and were not sourced in the notes.

## Backyard Worlds volunteers want WiseView controls, fast vetting and credit

Backyard Worlds' history is effectively a ten-year user study for your astronomy audience. The Zooniverse interface showed a 4-frame looping flipbook of unWISE difference images about 10′ across. W1 was mapped to blue and W2 to red, so methane-bearing cold objects look red, and volunteers clicked anything that moved in all four frames ([Kuchner et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/html/1705.02919.pdf)). Most of the teaching was artifact recognition. The field guide's rules of thumb are directly reusable. A real mover appears in every frame, and something in only two frames is probably a ghost. Many dipoles in one frame mean the telescope's pointing shifted. Stellar artifacts all "dance", so look for the one that moves differently ([BYW FAQ](https://www.zooniverse.org/projects/marckuchner/backyard-worlds-planet-9/about/faq)). Advanced volunteers moved off Zooniverse quickly to **WiseView**, a volunteer-built blinker. It adds any-coordinate access, adjustable field of view, blink-speed and zoom sliders, asinh stretch with "Trim Bright" clipping, W1/W2 band choice, and a Pan-STARRS comparison panel ([BYW blog, 2017](https://blog.backyardworlds.org/2017/09/13/wiseview/)). They then checked candidates in SIMBAD (1′ cone), VizieR (proper motion >100 mas/yr), IRSA Finder Chart and the Legacy Survey viewer before filing a "Think You've Got One?" form ([BYW FAQ](https://www.zooniverse.org/projects/marckuchner/backyard-worlds-planet-9/about/faq)). Every one of those manual hops is a feature you can wrap into one panel.

The project's own data show where efficiency lies. The 2023 spin-off Cool Neighbors switched to small 2′×2′ cutouts centred on machine-learning-ranked candidates, used no difference imaging, and asked a single yes/no question. Median time per classification fell from 22.0 s to 6.0 s, and median classifications per user rose from 15 to 31 ([Meisner et al. 2024](https://theoryandpractice.citizenscienceassociation.org/articles/10.5334/cstp.737)). Both projects have Gini coefficients above 0.8, meaning a small minority does most of the work. That argues for a two-tier design: a quick, centred "is something moving here?" mode for the public, and a full WiseView-style explorer with permalinks and exports for power users. The trade-off is real. "The Accident", a probable Y subdwarf, was found off the beaten path with a volunteer's custom software ([Space.com](https://www.space.com/the-accident-brown-dwarf-discovery-citizen-scientist)). Keep a free-roam mode.

Two findings bear directly on an AI guide. First, 77.4% of surveyed advanced participants were positive about ML/AI, but the team framed ML as a shortlister that runs before human vetting. Volunteer-facing text deliberately said "machine learning", not "AI", and stressed its limits ([Meisner et al. 2024](https://theoryandpractice.citizenscienceassociation.org/articles/10.5334/cstp.737)). Your guide should present itself the same way: it drives and explains, and the human decides. Note that only 53 of 359 advanced participants answered that survey. Second, **credit is the retention engine**. Volunteers made up 61 of the 75 authors on the 2026 catalog paper, and one co-author said "Yes, dreams do come true" ([NASA, May 2026](https://science.nasa.gov/get-involved/citizen-science/nasa-volunteers-double-known-population-of-brown-dwarfs/)). The project also keeps its subject data private so unpublished volunteer discoveries are not scooped ([Meisner et al. 2024](https://theoryandpractice.citizenscienceassociation.org/articles/10.5334/cstp.737)). A hackathon tool cannot promise co-authorship. It can give each candidate a timestamped "first flagged by" field and a clear path to the MPC or a science team.

SPHEREx's genuine gift to this community is spectra, not motion. Cold brown dwarfs show a deep CH4 trough at 3.3 µm and a flux peak at 4–5 µm. That gives them the very red W1−W2 colour WISE was designed around ([arXiv:1205.2122](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1205.2122)). SPHEREx samples both features, and it has already measured H2O, CO, CO2 and CH4 in 37 L0–Y4 brown dwarfs ([Rustamkulov et al., arXiv:2607.00543](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.00543)). A companion pipeline built a library of 6,003 ultracool dwarfs ([arXiv:2604.22012](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22012v1)). Meanwhile, Backyard Worlds' 3,006 new L/T candidates still need spectroscopy ([Schneider et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01323)). A "SPHEREx spectrum check" at any candidate position fills that gap. It would pull forced photometry from IRSA's Spectrophotometry Tool, compute a WISE-like F(4.5)/F(3.3) methane index, and overlay star, galaxy and T/Y templates. The notes do not establish how many of the faint BYW candidates are bright enough for SPHEREx, so present it as "check if detectable". Two caveats belong in the UI. Galaxy PAH *emission* at 3.3 µm can mimic or mask methane *absorption* in noisy R≈35 spectra. Shuttle glow also contaminates 3 and 4.5 µm ([Bock et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.02985v2)). The same colour logic also covers Planet Nine: the BYW FAQ says it would look red if methane is present and blue if methane has frozen out ([BYW FAQ](https://www.zooniverse.org/projects/marckuchner/backyard-worlds-planet-9/about/faq)).

## SPHEREx's own pixel flags make a credible vetting workflow possible

Vetting is where an astronomy user decides whether to trust the tool. SPHEREx makes it unusually tractable, because every Level-2 image carries a per-pixel FLAGS bitmap. The flags include TRANSIENT, OVERFLOW, NONFUNC, MISSING_DATA, HOT, COLD, NONLINEAR, PERSIST, OUTLIER and SOURCE ([IRSA SPHEREx intro tutorial](https://caltech-ipac.github.io/irsa-tutorials/spherex-intro/)). Archive documentation adds three classes of crosstalk ghost with per-detector masking tables in QR3 ([SPHEREx Archive docs](https://caltech-ipac.github.io/spherex-archive-documentation/spherex-data-products/)). Other mission-level systematics are stray-light halos around bright sources, shuttle glow, and particle hits that are elevated in the South Atlantic Anomaly ([Bock et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.02985v2)). The researchers propose a ten-step checklist the tool can mostly automate. Reject detections with bad flags within ~2 px. Require the source in two or more exposures or channels within a visit. Check against crosstalk offsets and bright-star halos. Confirm there is no Gaia, CatWISE or 2MASS counterpart. Rule out known solar-system objects at that epoch. Require positions to fit parallax plus slow orbital motion for a single distance. Check that flux is consistent across epochs. Finally, run an injection test showing a similar object would be detectable there. Known asteroids will dominate real movers near the ecliptic, since about 100,000 should have meaningful SPHEREx fluxes ([Bock et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.02985v2)). That makes the known-object check the step users will hit most often.

IRSA already offers SPHEREx services the app can call or link to. The **Moving Object Search Tool (MOST)** takes a name or orbital elements and returns which SPHEREx, WISE/NEOWISE, ZTF and other images cover the object's predicted path. That is precovery in one call ([IRSA MOST](https://irsa.ipac.caltech.edu/applications/MOST)). The JPL Horizons API returns ephemerides as JSON ([Horizons API](https://ssd-api.jpl.nasa.gov/doc/horizons.html)). Because SPHEREx's low-Earth-orbit parallax is only about 0.02″ at 500 AU, a geocentric ephemeris is good enough (researcher's calculation). TAP and the cutout service can assemble every epoch covering a position in time order ([IRSA cutouts tutorial](https://caltech-ipac.github.io/irsa-tutorials/spherex-cutouts/)). A credible exported report should hold per-epoch ICRS positions with uncertainties and mid-exposure times, plus OBSID, detector and channel. It should add motion rate and position angle, the implied distance range, photometry with errors, cutouts and flag summaries, cross-match links, and the data release DOI (QR3: 10.26131/IRSA662) ([IRSA QR overview](https://irsa.ipac.caltech.edu/data/SPHEREx/docs/overview_qr.html)). An MPC 80-column or ADES export and DS9 region files would let astronomers continue in their own tools. Find_Orb and the MPC formats are described from background knowledge (see the verification table).

Three engineering traps can sink a weekend build. First, a cutout keeps the **full, unmodified PSF HDU**, about 4.9 MB, even when the image stamp is ~13 KB. A small backend should strip it or cache one PSF per detector zone ([IRSA cutouts tutorial](https://caltech-ipac.github.io/irsa-tutorials/spherex-cutouts/)). Second, CORS support on IRSA and CDS endpoints is unconfirmed, so plan a proxy. Third, the IMAGE HDU is not zodi-subtracted; a modelled ZODI HDU is supplied separately ([Archive docs](https://caltech-ipac.github.io/spherex-archive-documentation/spherex-data-products/)). Some docs say "0.615 arcsec per pixel". That almost certainly refers to the 10×-oversampled PSF grid. Image pixels are 6.15″ ([IRSA Mosaic help](https://irsa.ipac.caltech.edu/onlinehelp/spherex/spherex/mosaic.html)).

## Prioritized features by audience

Rows marked "Differentiator" are absent from the competitor feature set you described (blink presets, aperture photometry, spectra, light curves, proper-motion fits with control stars, 102-channel browsing, AllWISE frames, scripted tours). "Table stakes" rows match what competitors already offer, so build them only to the level the differentiators depend on.

| Priority | Public audience | Astronomy / Backyard Worlds audience |

## Conclusion

The search for Planet Nine gives this tool something better than a target: a method visitors can actually perform, plus a live, unresolved question. Because SPHEREx almost certainly cannot settle that question, the most credible entry treats visitors as apprentices in the technique rather than prospectors for a prize. They blink like Tombaugh, measure a shift, turn it into a distance with the six-month quadrature baseline, and learn to reject a ghost. That reframing also resolves the apparent tension between your two audiences. The same parallax calculator, injection test and vetting checklist that give a member of the public a guaranteed and honest "I found it" are the tools a Backyard Worlds veteran would otherwise assemble from five websites.

The less obvious opportunity is spectroscopic. Backyard Worlds' thousands of candidates still need spectra, and SPHEREx now offers low-resolution 0.75–5 µm spectra across the whole sky. A methane-index check for any user-supplied position would make the tool useful to the citizen-science community after the hackathon ends. That kind of lasting contribution is what volunteers say motivates them most. With the event days away, build the honest framing and the planted-object tutorial first. They cost the least, separate you most clearly from blink-and-photometry competitors, and turn the scientific limitation into the story.
